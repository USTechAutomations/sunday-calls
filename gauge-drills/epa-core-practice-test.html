EPA 608 Core practice test: the ten Core topics explained

Core is the part of the Section 608 test that everyone sits, whatever equipment they work on. EPA lists ten Core topics. Below, each topic gets a plain sentence and the exact words from an official page.

Group I asks questions on the environmental impact of CFCs, HCFCs, and substitute refrigerants, on laws and regulations, and on the changing industry outlook.

The federal rule says: "Group I will ask questions in the following areas: 1. Environmental impact of CFCs, HCFCs, and substitute refrigerants 2. Laws and regulations 3. Changing industry outlook"

1. Environmental Impacts

This topic is about why the refrigerant rules exist: some refrigerants harm the ozone layer.

EPA's test outline lists this topic: "They move to the stratosphere and are broken down by strong ultraviolet (UV) light, where they release chlorine atoms that then deplete the ozone layer.".

EPA's page says: "They move to the stratosphere and are broken down by strong ultraviolet (UV) light, where they release chlorine atoms that then deplete the ozone layer."

3. Section 608 Regulations

This is the rulebook for handling refrigerant. It covers when you may not let refrigerant out, and who may buy it.

It is illegal to knowingly vent refrigerant while maintaining, servicing, repairing, or disposing of an appliance.

The federal rule says: "No person maintaining, servicing, repairing, or disposing of an appliance or industrial process refrigeration may knowingly vent or otherwise release into the environment any refrigerant from such appliances."

Read it on the official source

Tiny releases made while honestly trying to recover or recycle refrigerant (de minimis releases) are not a violation.

The federal rule says: "De minimis releases associated with good faith attempts to recycle or recover refrigerants are not subject to this prohibition."

Read it on the official source

An employer can buy refrigerant if it shows the seller proof that it employs at least one certified technician.

The federal rule says: "The buyer employs at least one technician who is certified as a Type I, Type II, Type III, or Universal technician under § 82.161 and provides proof of such to the seller;"

6. Three R Definitions

Recover, recycle and reclaim sound alike, but they are three different jobs. Learn the difference in words.

RECOVER means removing refrigerant in any condition and storing it in an external container without necessarily testing or processing it.

The federal rule says: "Recover means to remove refrigerant in any condition from an appliance and to store it in an external container without necessarily testing or processing it in any way."

Read it on the official source

Recycle means to extract refrigerant from an appliance, except motor-vehicle air conditioners, and clean it for reuse in equipment of the same owner without meeting all reclamation requirements.

The federal rule says: "Recycle, when referring to refrigerant, means to extract refrigerant from an appliance (except MVACs) and clean it for reuse in equipment of the same owner without meeting all of the requirements for reclamation."

Read it on the official source

A study order that works

Start with the words: the three R definitions and the refrigerant classes. Then learn the rules about releasing and buying refrigerant. Move on to recovery and evacuation. Leave safety and shipping for last and read them slowly, because they are full of small details.
