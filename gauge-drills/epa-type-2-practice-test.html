EPA 608 Type 2 practice test: high-pressure topics in plain words

Type 2 is the Section 608 certification for high-pressure equipment. This page explains what it covers, lists EPA's Type 2 topics, and walks through the leak repair rules in plain words. Each fact shows EPA's exact words and where to read them.

What Type 2 covers

The page describes Type II as for servicing or disposing of high- or very high-pressure appliances, except small appliances and motor-vehicle air conditioners.

EPA's page says: "For servicing or disposing of high- or very high-pressure appliances, except small appliances and MVACs (Type II)."

Read it on the official source

The regulation requires Type II certification for medium-, high- and very high-pressure appliances (except small appliances, MVACs and MVAC-like appliances).

The federal rule says: "Persons who maintain, service, repair, or dispose of medium-, high-, or very high-pressure appliances (except small appliances, MVACs, and MVAC-like appliances) must be certified as Type II technicians."

Read it on the official source

A high-pressure appliance uses a refrigerant with saturation pressure between 170 and 355 psia at 104 degrees F; examples include R-22, R-407A, R-407C, R-410A and R-502.

The federal rule says: "High-pressure appliance means an appliance that uses a refrigerant with a liquid phase saturation pressure between 170 psia and 355 psia at 104 °F. Examples include but are not limited to appliances using R-22, R-407A, R-407C, R-410A, and R-502."

Read it on the official source

A very high-pressure appliance uses a refrigerant with critical temperature below 104 degrees F or saturation pressure above 355 psia at 104 degrees F.

The federal rule says: "Very high-pressure appliance means an appliance that uses a refrigerant with a critical temperature below 104 °F or with a liquid phase saturation pressure above 355 psia at 104 °F."

Read it on the official source

Leak repair rules explained

Leak repair is the part that trips people up, so take it in small steps. First, which appliances the older rule covers. Then, how big a leak has to be before you must act. Then, how fast you must fix it and what you must write down.

The leak repair rules in Section 82.157 apply only to appliances with 50 or more pounds of ozone-depleting refrigerant, as of April 10, 2020.

The federal rule says: "As of April 10, 2020, this section applies only to appliances with a full charge of 50 or more pounds of any class I or class II refrigerant or blend containing a class I or class II refrigerant."

Read it on the official source

The requirements of this section do not apply to appliances containing solely substitute refrigerants.

The federal rule says: "the requirements of this section do not apply to appliances containing solely substitute refrigerants."

Read it on the official source

Leak-rate trigger for comfort cooling and other 50-pound-plus appliances is 10 percent.

The federal rule says: "10 percent leak rate for comfort cooling appliances or other appliances with a full charge of 50 or more pounds of refrigerant not covered by (c)(2)(i) or (ii) of this section."

Read it on the official source

Leaks must be identified and repaired within 30 days, or within 120 days if an industrial process shutdown is required.

The federal rule says: "must identify and repair leaks in accordance with this paragraph within 30 days (or 120 days if an industrial process shutdown is required)"

Read it on the official source

Leak repairs must be documented by both an initial and a follow-up verification test.

The federal rule says: "Repair of leaks must be documented by both an initial and a follow-up verification test or tests."

Read it on the official source

The follow-up verification test must be done within 10 days of the successful initial test or of the appliance reaching normal operating conditions.

The federal rule says: "A follow-up verification test must be performed within 10 days of the successful initial verification test or 10 days of the appliance reaching normal operating characteristics and conditions"

Read it on the official source

Leak repair records must be kept at least three years.

The federal rule says: "All records identified in this paragraph must be kept for at least three years in electronic or paper format, unless otherwise specified."

Read it on the official source

Very leaky equipment also has to be reported to EPA.

Appliances of 50+ pounds that leak 125 percent or more of full charge in a calendar year must be reported to EPA.

The federal rule says: "Owners or operators of appliances containing 50 pounds or more of refrigerant that leak 125 percent or more of the full charge in a calendar year must submit a report to EPA"

Read it on the official source

This report must be submitted by March 1 of the subsequent year.

The federal rule says: "This report must be submitted by March 1 of the subsequent year"

Read it on the official source

Common mix-ups between Type 2 and Type 3

Type 2 and Type 3 sound alike and both talk about vacuum, but they are different equipment. Learn the definitions side by side.

A high-pressure appliance uses a refrigerant with saturation pressure between 170 and 355 psia at 104 degrees F; examples include R-22, R-407A, R-407C, R-410A and R-502.

The federal rule says: "High-pressure appliance means an appliance that uses a refrigerant with a liquid phase saturation pressure between 170 psia and 355 psia at 104 °F. Examples include but are not limited to appliances using R-22, R-407A, R-407C, R-410A, and R-502."

Read it on the official source

A low-pressure appliance uses a refrigerant with saturation pressure below 45 psia at 104 degrees F; examples include R-11, R-123, R-113 and R-245fa.

The federal rule says: "Low-pressure appliance means an appliance that uses a refrigerant with a liquid phase saturation pressure below 45 psia at 104 °F. Examples include but are not limited to appliances using R-11, R-123, R-113, and R-245fa."

Read it on the official source

The units differ too. High-pressure vacuum levels are given in inches of mercury; low-pressure equipment is evacuated to a level given in millimetres of mercury, absolute.

The quoted row reads: high-pressure appliance, or isolated component, with a full charge of less than 200 pounds of refrigerant, 0 0.

EPA's page says: "High-pressure appliance, or isolated component of such appliance, with a full charge of less than 200 pounds of refrigerant 0 0"

Read it on the official source

The quoted row reads: low-pressure appliance, 25 mm Hg absolute, 25 mm Hg absolute.

EPA's page says: "Low-pressure appliance 25 mm Hg absolute 25 mm Hg absolute"

Read it on the official source

Even the first step of a small repair is opposite. A high-pressure appliance is emptied down to zero pounds per square inch gauge, while a low-pressure appliance is brought up to zero.

For a repair that is not major and not followed by evacuation to the environment, a medium-, high- or very high-pressure appliance need only be evacuated to 0 psig before opening.

The federal rule says: "Be evacuated to a pressure no higher than 0 psig before it is opened if it is a medium-, high- or very high-pressure appliance;"

Read it on the official source

For low-pressure appliances, the equipment must be pressurized to 0 psig before it is opened.

EPA's page says: "For low-pressure appliances, the equipment must be pressurized to 0 psig before it is opened."

Read it on the official source
