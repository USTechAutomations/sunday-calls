Before you start

  • Ask permission first. Tell your parent you would like to record, and why.
  • Pick a quiet room. Turn off the television and set your phone to silent.
  • Use the voice memo app on your phone. Lay it flat on a table between you, or hold the phone near their voice on a call.
  • Keep each session to about half an hour. One session a week is plenty.
  • Ask one question, then wait. Silence is often where the story starts.
  • If a tangent appears, follow it. The written questions are a map, not a script.
  • Name each file with the session number and the date, and save a second copy somewhere else.

Session 1: Childhood home

  1. What street or town did you grow up on, and what did the front door look like?
  2. Which room in that home did you spend the most time in?
  3. What sounds or smells still remind you of that house?
  4. Who lived under that roof with you, and who visited most?
  5. What chore was yours, and did you mind it?
  6. What did you do on a long summer day with nothing planned?

Session 2: School

  1. What was the walk or ride to school like?
  2. Which teacher do you still remember, and why?
  3. Who was your closest friend then, and what did you two get up to?
  4. What did you once get in trouble for at school?
  5. What did you daydream about during class?
  6. Whose opinion of you mattered most in those years?

Session 3: First job

  1. What was the first thing you ever earned money for?
  2. How did you find your first real job, and how did the first morning feel?
  3. Who showed you how the work was done?
  4. What did your first paycheck go toward?
  5. Which workplace felt most like a second home?
  6. What skill from your working years do you still use today?

Session 4: Love

  1. Who was your first crush, and what became of it?
  2. Where were you the day you knew you were in love?
  3. How did you tell your family about the one you chose?
  4. What did the day you committed to each other feel like?
  5. Which small habits of your partner did you come to treasure?
  6. What did you learn about love from watching your own parents?

Session 5: Hard years

  1. Which year would you call your hardest, and what made it so?
  2. Who showed up for you when things were bad?
  3. What did you do to keep going on the hardest days?
  4. What did you have to give up along the way?
  5. What did that time teach you that nothing else could have?
  6. What would you say to someone going through the same thing today?

Session 6: Proud moments

  1. What did you build, make, or fix that you still think about?
  2. When did you feel most trusted by others?
  3. What did you accomplish that went unnoticed by everyone except you?
  4. Which day would you pick to live all over again?
  5. What did you do for someone else that you are glad you did?
  6. Who do you hope would be proud of you, and why?

Session 7: Advice

  1. What is one mistake you would happily warn a younger person about?
  2. What do you wish someone had told you at twenty?
  3. Which habit has served you well for a lifetime?
  4. How do you decide what matters when everything feels urgent?
  5. What would you tell your grandchildren about money, work, or time?
  6. What do you know now that you did not know at forty?

Session 8: Anything left unsaid

  1. Is there a thank-you you have never said out loud?
  2. Is there an apology you have carried for a long time?
  3. Which story do you worry will be forgotten?
  4. What would you like read aloud at a family gathering years from now?
  5. Who could we ask about the parts of the story you do not know?
  6. If you could add one last page to this book, what would it say?

After the eight sessions

Listen back once, and write down the stories you would not want to lose. You can type them up yourself, or keep the recordings as they are. Some families print the most-loved answers for a holiday gathering.
